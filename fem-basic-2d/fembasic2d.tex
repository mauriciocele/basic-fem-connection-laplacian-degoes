\title[From the Vector Heat Equation to Code]
 {Parallel Transport of Tangent Vectors on Triangle Meshes:\\ from the Vector Heat Equation to Code, with Finite Elements}

\author[Mauricio Cele Lopez Belon]{Mauricio Cele Lopez Belon}
\address{Madrid, Spain}
\email{mclopez@outlook.com}

\subjclass{65N30 Finite element methods; 53A05 Surfaces in Euclidean space; 65F05 Direct methods for linear systems}
\keywords{Finite Element Method, Connection Laplacian, Levi-Civita Connection, Parallel Transport, Vector Heat Method}
\date{October 25, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We derive, step by step, a program that spreads a tangent vector given at one point of a triangle mesh to every other point, so that the result at each point is the parallel transport of the given vector. The method is the vector heat method: diffuse the vector by the heat equation for tangent vector fields, whose operator is the connection Laplacian, and keep only the direction of the result. We start from the partial differential equation and go all the way down to the linear system that the code solves: the covariant derivative and the Levi-Civita connection of a surface, Green's identity for tangent fields, the backward Euler step, the weak form, the finite element space of piecewise linear tangent fields, the discrete parallel transport that makes that space possible, the gradients of the shape functions on a triangle embedded in space, the element stiffness and mass matrices, their assembly, the boundary condition, and the solution. Every step is motivated, every formula is derived, and every formula that the code evaluates is matched with the function that evaluates it, so that the text can be read side by side with the implementation \cite{ConnectionLaplacianFEM}. A second part derives the same method in geometric algebra, where the tangent plane is a bivector, coordinates are complex numbers of that plane, parallel transport is a rolling rotor and the connection Laplacian becomes a complex Hermitian matrix, and it clarifies which parts of the computation are intrinsic to the curved surface. A third part removes the last extrinsic ingredient, the tangent plane of a vertex, and builds the whole method from the edge lengths of the mesh alone: the metric of a face is its Gram matrix, the plane of a vertex is the cone of its angles rescaled to a full turn, and the connection matches the bisectors of the corners; the result is exact on bent and folded meshes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{section:introduction}

Suppose we are given one tangent vector at one point of a curved surface, and we want to know what that vector ``is'' at every other point: the vector we would obtain by carrying it along the surface without turning it. On a plane the answer is trivial, the same vector everywhere. On a curved surface it is not, for two reasons. First, the tangent planes at different points are different planes, so the vector cannot simply be copied; it has to be carried, and the rule that says how is the \emph{parallel transport} of the surface, given by its \emph{Levi-Civita connection}. Second, the result depends on the path: carrying a vector around a closed loop on a sphere brings it back rotated, by an angle equal to the curvature enclosed. A sensible global answer is to carry the vector along the shortest path from the source, the geodesic.

The \emph{vector heat method} of Sharp, Soliman and Crane \cite{Sharp2019} computes that answer without computing a single geodesic. Its idea is simple. Heat spreading from a point source travels, for a short time, mostly along shortest paths. If what spreads is not a temperature but a tangent vector, which diffuses by the heat equation for tangent vector fields, then the direction of the diffused vector at each point is the direction of the source vector carried along the shortest path; only its length decays with distance. So: diffuse the vector for a short time, and normalize.

This text derives a finite element implementation of that idea \cite{ConnectionLaplacianFEM}, following the classical presentations of the finite element method \cite{Sayas2015, Langtangen2014} and extending them from scalar functions on flat domains to tangent vector fields on surfaces. The derivation has a fixed structure, which recurs in every finite element method and which we follow strictly:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the continuous problem: the operator, the equation, the boundary condition (Sections~\ref{section:geometry}--\ref{section:pde});
\item the weak form, which needs only first derivatives (Section~\ref{section:weak});
\item a finite dimensional space of candidate solutions, the \emph{trial space}, and of \emph{test functions} (Section~\ref{section:galerkin});
\item the element matrices, computed triangle by triangle (Sections~\ref{section:gradients}--\ref{section:element});
\item the assembly of the global matrices, the boundary condition, and the solution of the linear system (Sections~\ref{section:assembly}--\ref{section:solve}).
\end{enumerate}
The ingredient that is not in the scalar method is the connection: tangent vectors at different vertices live in different planes and cannot be added before they are brought into a common plane. Section~\ref{section:transport} shows how the code does that, and Section~\ref{section:galerkin} shows that this is exactly where the Levi-Civita connection enters the finite element method. Table~\ref{tab:map} at the end of Part~\ref{part:matrix} lists every formula with the function of \code{FEM.cpp} that evaluates it. Part~\ref{part:ga} repeats the derivation in the language of geometric algebra, where frames become a single vector, coordinates become complex numbers of the tangent plane, transports become rotors, and the gradients of the shape functions become a reciprocal frame; it also separates what is intrinsic to the curved surface from what depends on its embedding in space. Part~\ref{part:intrinsic} builds the same method from the edge lengths alone, slowly and with every derivation, and compares the two connections on a folded sheet, a sphere and the cactus.

\paragraph{How to read the formulas.} The text is meant to be read in pieces, on different days, without having to turn back. Two conventions help. Whenever an earlier equation is used, its number is followed by the equation itself in square brackets, for example ``by~(\ref{eqn:frame})~[\,$T^TT = I_2$\,]''; the bracket repeats the part that is needed, sometimes in a shortened form. And derivations are written out step by step, in the style of a school textbook: every substitution is shown, every identity that is used is named or proved on the spot, and when an expression is rewritten into a new form the intermediate lines are kept. A reader who already knows a step can skip it; a reader who does not will find it there.
